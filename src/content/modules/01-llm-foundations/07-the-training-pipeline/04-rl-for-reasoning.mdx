<Subsection title="A different kind of reward signal: correctness, not preference" variant="a">

[Preference training](/01-llm-foundations/07-the-training-pipeline/03-preference-training-and-message-roles/#beyond-correct-format-which-response-is-actually-better)
improves which *style* or *approach* of response humans (or an AI
judge) prefer. It doesn't specifically train a model to reason through a
problem step by step before answering. **RL for reasoning** is a newer
training stage built for exactly that: training a model, via
reinforcement learning, to generate extended intermediate reasoning
before producing a final answer — with the reward signal here often
being something more objective than human preference: whether the
*final answer* is actually correct.

[The pipeline so far](/01-llm-foundations/07-the-training-pipeline/01-pretraining/#what-actually-produces-the-parameters-behind-every-logit),

<Subsection title="Why correctness works as a reward signal here" variant="b">

For domains with objectively checkable answers — math problems, code
that either passes tests or doesn't, logic puzzles with one correct
solution — you don't need a human to manually judge each response the
way
[preference training does](/01-llm-foundations/07-the-training-pipeline/03-preference-training-and-message-roles/#rlhf-rlaif-and-dpo-different-ways-of-using-the-same-idea).
You can automatically check whether the final answer, after a chain of
reasoning steps, is actually correct, and use that as a reward signal
reinforcing whichever reasoning patterns tend to *lead to* correct
answers. This is a genuinely more scalable, automatable training signal
than human preference judgments, at least for the specific domains where
"correct" is unambiguous and checkable. Here is what one such training
problem looks like — a math word problem with a known final answer, and

<Subsection title="Where this leads directly: reasoning models and test-time compute" variant="a">

This training stage is precisely what produces what get called
**reasoning models** — models that, when actually generating a
response, produce a substantial amount of intermediate reasoning text
before their final answer, because that's exactly the behavior this
training stage rewarded. And
[every one of those intermediate reasoning tokens is a real, full generation step](/01-llm-foundations/04-how-llms-generate-text/02-autoregressive-generation/#the-model-never-generates-more-than-one-token-at-once)
— the same computational cost as any other token,
[covered back in Lesson 4](/01-llm-foundations/04-how-llms-generate-text/02-autoregressive-generation/#why-this-matters-computationally).
This directly explains why reasoning models cost more and take longer
to respond: they're not doing something mysteriously different per
token, they're simply generating *more* tokens — the reasoning itself —
before reaching their answer. The practical tradeoffs of this, and when
it's actually worth the extra cost, are covered directly in
[the next lesson](/01-llm-foundations/08-scaling-laws-and-emergent-behavior/04-test-time-compute/)
under **test-time compute** — this concept's job is only
establishing where that behavior actually comes from: a specific,
deliberate training stage, not an emergent accident.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="The same idea, applied to agents" variant="b">

Correct final answers aren't the only thing that can be checked
automatically. So can a finished task: did the code's tests pass after
the model's changes, did the file end up with the right contents, did
the booking in a simulated system get made correctly? Labs increasingly
run reinforcement learning on exactly this kind of **multi-step agentic
task**. The model works inside an environment with real tools, calling
them over many turns, and is rewarded when the task is completed
correctly.

This is where much of a modern model's skill as an agent comes from:
deciding which tool to call next, reading a tool's error and trying
something different, knowing when the task is done. It's also why
agent benchmarks, which score models on completing realistic tasks with
tools rather than answering questions, have become a standard part of
how new models are compared. When you choose a model for an agent, those
results often tell you more than general chat quality does.

</Subsection>
