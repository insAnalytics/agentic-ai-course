<Subsection title="Closing synthesis — reason it through, then check" variant="a">

This isn't graded — it's a chance to apply this lesson's mechanics to
scenarios not already covered, then verify with the same real tool from
the opening concept.

For each scenario below, reason through three questions before
checking: **(a)** roughly what would the next-token distribution look
like — one dominant candidate, or several closely competing? **(b)** is
hallucination risk high or low here, and why? **(c)** is knowledge
cutoff relevant to this specific prompt at all?

1. `"The opposite of hot is very ___"` — completing with a temperature word.
2. `"The best programming language for beginners is ___"` — completing with a language name.
3. `"The winner of next year's Nobel Prize in Physics will be Dr. ___"` — completing with a surname.
4. `"The current CEO of Twitter is Jack ___"` — completing with a surname.

*Reasoning to check against:* (1) should have one clearly dominant
candidate (`"cold"`, at roughly a third of the total probability, well
ahead of anything else) — a near-universal, unambiguous fact, low
hallucination risk, cutoff irrelevant. (2) should show several closely
competing candidates (`"Python"` narrowly ahead, `"C"`/`"Java"`/`"Ruby"`
close behind) — genuine disagreement exists in training data itself,
since this is actually a matter of opinion, not a hallucination risk so
much as an inherently contested question.

(3) is worth pausing on, because the real tool tells a more interesting
story here than "confident but wrong" — the actual distribution is
**flat**: no candidate surname reaches even 3%, and the top several are
all within a hair of each other. This is still
[exactly the same territory as the model-predicts-it-doesn't-know concept](/01-llm-foundations/04-how-llms-generate-text/03-the-model-predicts-it-doesnt-know/)
— there's no real answer to draw on, so the mechanism is still just
guessing — but here the guess comes out *honestly* uncertain rather than
falsely confident, unlike the fabricated-city example or the Einstein
misconception from the hallucination concept. The lesson to take from the contrast: false
confidence specifically needs a misleading pattern strong enough in
training data to dominate (a popular misconception, a memorized name tied
to a role) — a genuinely open, arbitrary unknown doesn't always produce
one. (4) is the cleanest possible demonstration of knowledge cutoff:
`"Dorsey"` comes out at essentially `100%` confidence — because Jack
Dorsey really was Twitter's CEO throughout GPT-2's training data. Whether
that's still true by the time you're reading this is exactly the point:
the model has no way to know if it isn't.

The pattern to walk away with: the *shape* of the distribution — one
dominant token, several competing ones, or a flat spread — is often a
more honest signal of how settled an answer actually is than the fluency
of the final generated sentence ever reveals on its own.

</Subsection>
