<Subsection title="A separate problem from computation and cost" variant="a">

Everything covered so far in this lesson —
[the context window's hard limit](/01-llm-foundations/06-context-windows-and-kv-cache/01-what-a-context-window-is/#what-happens-when-you-exceed-it),
[KV cache](/01-llm-foundations/06-context-windows-and-kv-cache/03-kv-cache/),
[prompt structure's effect on cost](/01-llm-foundations/06-context-windows-and-kv-cache/04-prompt-structure-and-cache-hits/)
— is about computation: how much work is required, and how efficiently
it gets done. There's a separate, distinct problem worth naming
directly: even when a model *can* fit a large amount of content into its
context window and process it efficiently, it doesn't necessarily *use*
every part of that content equally reliably.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="The lost-in-the-middle effect" variant="b">

This is a well-documented, empirically observed pattern: models tend to
recall and actually use information sitting near the **beginning** or
**end** of a long context far more reliably than information buried
somewhere in the **middle** — even when that middle-positioned
information is exactly as relevant and clearly stated as content at
either edge.

Below is real published data on exactly this, from the study that named
the effect (Liu et al., 2023). Each model was asked the same kind of
question over 20 retrieved documents, with exactly one document
containing the answer — and the *only* thing that changed between
measurements was where in the 20 that answer-containing document sat.
Pick a model to see its accuracy at documents 1, 5, 10, 15, and 20:

<PositionAccuracyChart client:load />

`GPT-3.5-Turbo` draws the cleanest U: `75.8%` correct when the answer is
in the first document, down to `53.8%` when it's in the tenth, back up to
`63.2%` when it's in the last — and that middle result is actually
*lower* than the same model's `56.1%` accuracy with no documents at all
(the dashed line), meaning the extra context did more harm than good for
that position. Not every model draws the same curve, though: `Claude-1.3`
and `MPT-30B-Instruct` show much shallower dips, and `LongChat-13B` shows
mostly a strong advantage for the very start with little recovery at the
end. What holds across all four is the underlying point — the best
accuracy always sits at one of the two edges, and the same fact placed in
the middle is used less reliably.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Why this happens — with appropriate honesty about what's settled" variant="a">

It's worth being direct that there isn't one single, fully agreed-upon
mechanistic explanation for exactly why this pattern occurs — plausible
contributing factors researchers point to include how attention and
positional encoding interact over very long sequences, and the fact that
real training documents often genuinely do concentrate their most
important content near their own beginnings and endings (introductions
and conclusions), which the model may have picked up as a general
pattern. The empirical pattern itself is well-documented and consistent
across many studies; the precise causal story behind *why* it happens
remains a more open question — the same kind of honest hedge
[already applied to what individual transformer layers represent, back in Lesson 3](/01-llm-foundations/03-attention-and-transformer-architecture/04-stacking-layers-into-a-transformer/#what-different-layers-tend-to-capture-with-real-caution).

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Why this matters practically" variant="b">

A large context window is not a guarantee that every token inside it
gets used equally well — this is exactly the risk for an agent that
stuffs a long conversation history, or a large document, into context
and simply assumes the model will reliably notice and use anything
relevant, wherever it happens to sit. A critical instruction or fact
buried deep in the middle of a large context is at genuine, documented
risk of being effectively ignored, even though it's fully present and
technically within the context window the whole time. Deliberately
placing the most important content near the beginning or end, when
possible, is a reasonable practical response to this — full prompt
structuring technique for working around it belongs to the later prompt
engineering scope in the Agentic AI basics module, but the underlying
reason to care about it at all is exactly this empirical pattern.

</Subsection>
