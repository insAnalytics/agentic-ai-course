<Subsection title={'From "looks close on a plot" to an actual number'} variant="a">

Every embedding concept so far has relied on a visual, geometric
intuition — points close together on a plot. **Cosine similarity** is
the real, computable operation underneath that intuition: a single
number, between `-1` and `1`, measuring how similar two vectors actually
are.

The key idea: cosine similarity measures the **angle** between two
vectors, not the raw distance between their endpoints. Two vectors
pointing in almost the same direction get a similarity near `1`, even if
one vector happens to be much longer than the other. Two vectors
pointing in completely opposite directions get a similarity near `-1`.
Two vectors at a right angle to each other — genuinely unrelated — get a
similarity near `0`. This matters specifically because an embedding
vector's *length* isn't meaningful on its own — what actually encodes
similarity is *direction*, which is exactly what angle, and therefore
cosine similarity, captures.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="The actual computation" variant="b">

```python
import math

def dot_product(vec_a: list, vec_b: list) -> float:
    return sum(vec_a[i] * vec_b[i] for i in range(len(vec_a)))

def magnitude(vec: list) -> float:
    return math.sqrt(sum(x ** 2 for x in vec))

def cosine_similarity(vec_a: list, vec_b: list) -> float:
    return dot_product(vec_a, vec_b) / (magnitude(vec_a) * magnitude(vec_b))
```

`dot_product` multiplies each pair of matching positions and sums the
results — [a comprehension over `range(len(...))`, exactly the indexed-pairing pattern from the data structures lesson](/00-python-essentials/02-collections/05-comprehensions/).
`magnitude` computes a vector's own length. Dividing the dot product by
both magnitudes is what removes length from the equation entirely,
leaving only the angle between the two directions — the actual formula
behind "how close is the meaning," not just "how big are these numbers."

</Subsection>

These are [the exact three toy embeddings from the token-embeddings concept](/01-llm-foundations/02-embeddings/02-token-embeddings-internal-input-layer/) —
`"cat"` and `"kitten"` produce a similarity close to `1` (≈`0.998`),
confirming what looked true visually is also true numerically; `"cat"`
and `"car"` produce a similarity close to `0` (≈`-0.108`), confirming
they're essentially unrelated in direction. This is precisely the
operation [a text-embedding-based search — the RAG groundwork from the previous concept — actually runs](/01-llm-foundations/02-embeddings/03-text-embeddings-a-distinct-use-case/),
at scale: computing cosine similarity between a query's embedding and
many candidate texts' embeddings, to find whichever ones are closest in
meaning. One thing to expect with real embedding models: "unrelated"
pairs rarely score near 0. They often land somewhere around 0.1–0.3 (the
exact baseline varies by model), and negative scores are rare. What
matters is the comparison: related pairs score clearly higher than
unrelated ones.
