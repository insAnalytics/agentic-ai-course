export const RULE_DEMO = String.raw`# a migration task as five steps: how often each goes wrong, and what a wrong step costs if nothing
# catches it, in the same made-up units as the check's cost
steps = [
    ("look up the agent's record",        0.03, 1),
    ("search the migration runbook",      0.05, 1),
    ("draft the migration plan",          0.05, 5),
    ("change the agent's model",          0.02, 200),
    ("post a summary to the team",        0.03, 80),
]
# one kind of check: catches 90% of wrong steps, costs 1 unit to run, and wrongly blocks 2% of
# correct steps, each false block costing 3 units (a retry, or a person looking at it)
CATCHES, COST, FALSE_BLOCKS, FALSE_BLOCK_COST = 0.90, 1.0, 0.02, 3.0

total_all = total_chosen = 0.0
for name, wrong, harm in steps:
    saved = wrong * harm * CATCHES
    spent = COST + (1 - wrong) * FALSE_BLOCKS * FALSE_BLOCK_COST
    worth = saved > spent
    total_all += saved - spent
    total_chosen += (saved - spent) if worth else 0.0
    print(f"{name:<30} saves {saved:5.2f}, costs {spent:4.2f}  ->  {'check it' if worth else 'skip'}")
print()
print(f"net gain, checking every step:         {total_all:5.2f}")
print(f"net gain, checking only where it pays: {total_chosen:5.2f}")
`;

<Subsection title="Checking everything is its own failure" variant="a">

[The first concept in this lesson](/06-reliability/02-reliability-tradeoffs/01-four-things-every-technique-trades/)
showed that every check costs latency, calls and false blocks.
[Lesson 1's arithmetic](/06-reliability/01-per-step-reliability/01-small-errors-compound/)
shows why that matters at the scale of an agent: a check on every one of
twenty steps pays its cost twenty times, and its false blocks compound the
same way errors do. An agent that verifies everything uniformly can end up
slower, more expensive and more likely to refuse than one with no checks at
all, while spending most of its effort on steps where a mistake was cheap.

The alternative is to spend unevenly: more where a mistake is costly, less
where it isn't. This is a design principle rather than a measured law, so
here's what supports it, and then how to apply it.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="The evidence: uneven spending beats even spending" variant="b">

No study has measured "reliability spending by risk" for agents directly.
The closest evidence is about spending effort unevenly by something else:

- **By difficulty.** Snell et al.,
  [*Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314)
  (2024), found that how much extra computation helps depends heavily on how
  hard a prompt is. Choosing the strategy and amount per prompt, based on
  its difficulty, matched a uniform best-of-N baseline while using about four
  times less computation.
- **By a failed check.** In Kapoor et al.'s HumanEval results from the
  previous concept, the "Escalation" baseline ran a cheap model first and
  moved up to more expensive models only when the code failed its example
  tests. It reached 85.0% accuracy at $0.27 for the whole benchmark, against
  89.6% at $1.93 for calling GPT-4 on every problem: a bit less accurate, at
  about a seventh of the cost. Lesson 6 covers this kind of escalation.

Neither is about risk. Both show the same thing, which is what the principle
rests on: effort spent only where it's needed buys more than the same effort
spread evenly.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="What makes a step risky" variant="a">

Module 3 already drew the first line:
[not all tools carry the same risk](/03-tool-design-for-agents/11-designing-for-least-privilege/02-separate-reads-from-writes-and-gate-the-writes/#not-all-tools-carry-the-same-risk),
and writes whose mistakes are costly get a gate. For reliability, four
properties raise the cost of a wrong step:

- **It can't be undone.** A deleted record, a payment, a sent message. A
  wrong read can be repeated; a wrong irreversible action can only be
  compensated for, if at all. Lesson 7 is about these steps.
- **It reaches outside the system.** Emails, posts, calls to other
  services: anything other people or systems see and act on.
- **The user sees it and acts on it.** A final answer that states a figure
  or cites a source is where an unsupported claim does its damage. Lesson 4
  checks those.
- **It comes after untrusted input.** Once the agent has read a web page or
  a retrieved document, what it does next may have been steered by it.
  Lesson 9 restricts what's allowed from that point.

Two consequences follow.

**Check at the point of no return.** Because mistakes propagate, a wrong
read early in a run usually does its damage later, at the first
irreversible step. A check just before that step can catch the result of
many earlier mistakes at once, which is often cheaper than checking each of
them.

**Cheap steps can go unchecked.** Reading a record, searching a runbook or
drafting a plan that the agent will act on later are all steps where a
mistake shows up downstream and costs little to redo.

</Subsection>

With these numbers, checking every step still comes out slightly ahead of
not checking at all, but checking only the two risky steps gains nearly five
times as much, because the three cheap steps each cost more to check than
they could save.

The rule is only as good as its inputs, and they're hard to get. The harm of
a wrong step is often a judgement, and a check's catch rate and false-block
rate have to be measured, as the first concept's habit says. So treat it as
a way of reasoning about where to spend, not a formula to trust blindly.
When the numbers are unknown, the four properties above are a reasonable
default: gate the irreversible, the external and the user-facing, and leave
the rest light.

</Subsection>
