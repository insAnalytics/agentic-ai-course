<Subsection title="The signals, and what each costs" variant="a">

Partway through a task, an agent needs some sign that its latest answer or
step is doubtful before it can decide to stop, ask or escalate. The course
has already built several such signals:

| Signal | Where it came from | What it costs | Available when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Token probabilities (logprobs) | [Module 1](/01-llm-foundations/05-decoding-strategies-and-generation-controls/05-logprobs/) | Nothing extra, if the provider returns them | The provider supports them |
| Agreement among samples | [Lesson 5](/06-reliability/05-voting/03-agreement-as-a-confidence-signal/) | One call per extra sample | The answer can be compared exactly |
| A failed check against sources | [Lesson 4](/06-reliability/04-verifying-claims/01-checking-each-claim/) | A judge call per claim | There are sources to check against |
| A low retrieval score | [Module 5](/05-rag-systems/10-grounded-answers/03-abstaining-when-retrieval-is-weak/) | Nothing extra | The answer rests on retrieval |
| The model's stated confidence | Asking the model | A few output tokens | Always, but see below |

Module 1 showed what a logprob is and promised that using one as a
confidence signal would come later. That's where this concept starts.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Where logprobs are available" variant="b">

Not every provider returns them. OpenAI's Chat Completions API does when
asked, through its `logprobs` and `top_logprobs` parameters, as
[its documentation shows](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/using_logprobs),
though not for every model: its reasoning models generally don't return them.
Open models served with engines such as vLLM, which produced this module's
runs, return them too. Anthropic's Messages API has no such option: its
[reference for creating a message](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/messages/create)
lists no parameter for them, and its responses carry none. A signal that
depends on logprobs ties the agent to providers that offer them.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Logprobs on an answer written after reasoning" variant="a">

Every reply in Lesson 1's runs ended with an `ANSWER:` line, and the runs
stored the probability of each token on that line. Multiplying them gives
the probability the model gave the whole answer line. Here's how that
relates to being right:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={ANSWER_DATA} setupCode={LOAD_UNSURE} code={ANSWER_LINE_DEMO} />

*(Real committed runs; a few replies with no answer line have no
probability and are left out. `load_run`, `load_set` and the grading
functions from Lesson 5 are already loaded for every demo in this lesson.)*

The relationship is weak, and it doesn't run the way a confidence signal
should. For the 2B, answers written with 99% or more probability were right
92.8% of the time, and answers in the 50–90% band were right more often,
97.7%. Only the lowest band, about one answer in twenty, was clearly worse,
at 77.6%. For the 4B, the least probable answers were all right. Two things explain this:

- **The probability is of an exact string, not an answer.** A correct
  `GET /agents/me` can come out at 55%, because the model was nearly as
  likely to write the same endpoint as `/agents/me`. Two ways of writing one
  answer split the probability between them.
- **By the answer line, the decision is already made.** The reply reasons
  first and then writes its conclusion. If the reasoning went wrong, the
  wrong conclusion is written with near-certainty: the 2B's wrong answer
  "2", to a question whose answer is 3, was written with over 95%
  probability every time. The probability measures
  how sure the model is of copying out its conclusion, not how sure it is of
  the conclusion.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Logprobs on a one-word decision" variant="b">

The support and contradiction judges from Lesson 4 are different: each
reply is a single verdict word, and that word is the decision. The runs
stored its probability too:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={JUDGE_DATA} setupCode={MATH_SETUP} code={VERDICT_DEMO} />

*(Real committed judge runs; "under 0.9" means the judge gave its chosen
verdict word less than a 90% chance. `math` from the demo above is already
imported here.)*

Here the signal is strong. The 4B judge's three wrong support verdicts all
came with less than 90% probability, as did 14 of its 15 wrong contradiction
verdicts, while only about one right verdict in six did. Flagging verdicts
under 0.9 for a second look would have caught nearly all the mistakes at the
cost of rechecking some right ones. These are small numbers from two models,
but the contrast with the answer-line probabilities is large.

The practical rule that follows: read the probability where the decision
is made. On a one-word classification, such as a verdict, a label or a
route, the token *is* the decision. On an answer written after reasoning,
it isn't, and the signal has to come from elsewhere: agreement between
samples, or a check against sources.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Measuring a signal on your own cases" variant="a">

A signal earns its place the way
[Lesson 4's judges did](/06-reliability/04-verifying-claims/02-checking-the-checker/):
by being measured on cases whose answers are known. Two measurements do most
of the work. The first compares signals with one number, the **AUROC**.
Pick one right answer and one wrong answer at random. The AUROC is the chance
the signal scores the right one higher. At 0.5 the signal is a coin toss, and
at 1.0 it separates right from wrong perfectly. The second picks a threshold,
and shows what it costs.

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={SIGNALS_DATA} setupCode={SIGNALS_SETUP} code={SIGNALS_DEMO} />

*(Real committed runs. `answer_probability` and `verdict_probability` from
the demos above are already loaded here.)*

The AUROC puts the earlier findings on one scale. For the 2B, the probability
of an answer line written after reasoning is almost exactly a coin toss.
Agreement among five samples, from
[Lesson 5](/06-reliability/05-voting/03-agreement-as-a-confidence-signal/),
is much better. A judge's one-word verdict is better still. The ranking is
what matters. None of these numbers carries over to another task unmeasured,
and 8 wrong votes and 23 wrong verdicts are small samples, as
[Lesson 4's rule of three](/06-reliability/04-verifying-claims/02-checking-the-checker/#zero-errors-isn-t-a-zero-error-rate)
warned.

The AUROC doesn't choose a threshold. It only says whether a good one
exists. The sweep does the choosing, and turns it into
[Lesson 2's trade](/06-reliability/02-reliability-tradeoffs/04-spend-reliability-where-the-risk-is/).
Reviewing the judges' verdicts under 0.9 means a second look at nearly one in
five, and catches all but two of the mistakes. Under 0.95 catches every one, at
a quarter of all verdicts. Which is right depends on what a missed mistake
costs against what a review costs.
[Module 5 chose a retrieval threshold](/05-rag-systems/10-grounded-answers/03-abstaining-when-retrieval-is-weak/#choosing-a-threshold)
the same way.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="What about asking the model?" variant="b">

The research on stated confidence disagrees with itself. Tian et al.,
[*Just Ask for Calibration*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14975) (EMNLP 2023),
found that for models trained with human feedback, including ChatGPT, GPT-4
and Claude, confidence stated in words was typically better calibrated than
the models' token probabilities, often halving the calibration error.
[Xiong et al.](/06-reliability/05-voting/03-agreement-as-a-confidence-signal/)
found stated confidence overconfident, with agreement among samples helping.
Both can be true on different models, tasks and prompts. This module's runs
didn't ask for stated confidence, so there's no local evidence either way.

The safe conclusion covers every signal in this concept, stated confidence
included: none can be trusted until it's been measured on labelled cases
from your own task, the way the previous section measured three of them.
Judged the Lesson 2 way, each signal is a check with a catch rate and a
false-flag rate. Measuring how well confidence scores match accuracy across
a whole system, calibration, is Module 7's subject.

</Subsection>
