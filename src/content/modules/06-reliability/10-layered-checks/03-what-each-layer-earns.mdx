<Subsection title="Each layer on its own" variant="a">

[Lesson 2](/06-reliability/02-reliability-tradeoffs/01-four-things-every-technique-trades/)
said a technique is judged by what it catches, what it wrongly blocks, and
what it costs. Here's each layer alone on the suite, then all of them
together:

<LiveDemo client:load code={ALONE_DEMO} setupCode={SETUP} />

*(Counts on the scripted suite; "blocked" means a fine scenario stopped.)*

No single layer comes close to the whole stack. The best of them alone stop
two of the eight harms; all of them together stop every one. The three that
stop nothing on their own, the result check, the read-back and the
missing-part check, are the pairs from the previous concept. The first two
turn a failure into an error, and the missing-part check refuses an answer
that ignores it. The costs are small and specific: the read-back adds a read
after every write, the session guard asks a person once (the legitimate
change made after reading the documents), and the support judge makes one
model call per cited claim. The stand-in for that person approves whatever
it's asked, the worst case for a busy reviewer. No harmful scenario here
reaches it, since the guard denies both planted emails outright. An
approval step only protects anything if the person actually checks.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="What each layer adds to the others" variant="b">

"Alone" undersells layers that work with others and oversells ones that
overlap. The fairer question is what the stack loses when one layer is taken
out:

<LiveDemo client:load code={WITHOUT_DEMO} setupCode={SETUP} />

Every layer is the only thing standing between the agent and at least one
harm in this suite, and the missing-part check between it and two. Only two
layers cause wrong blocks, and taking either out removes its false block and
lets its harm through: the grounding check trades the derived-count answer for
catching the invented figure, and the intent check trades the loosely named
agent for catching the invented argument. Whether each trade is worth it is
the call
[Lesson 2](/06-reliability/02-reliability-tradeoffs/03-equal-budgets-need-a-stated-unit/)
said needs a stated unit: a wrongly withheld count costs the user a retry; an
invented argument costs a changed record.

This suite was built so that each harm has a layer aimed at it, which is why
every layer earns its place here. A real suite would find overlaps, a layer
whose every catch another layer also makes, and that's the evidence for
dropping one. Building suites like this, and running them as the agent
changes, is Module 7's subject.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="What the model-based layer really costs and catches" variant="a">

Seven of the eight layers are code: they cost nothing to run, and on a given
input they do the same thing every time. The support judge is the exception,
and its numbers shouldn't come from a scripted verdict. Here's what the real
judge cost per call in Lesson 4's run:

<LiveDemo client:load code={JUDGE_COST_DEMO} dataFiles={JUDGE_DATA} />

*(From the committed Lesson 4 run on one Colab G4 GPU.)*

So the suite's two judge calls stand for about 500 prompt tokens and 25 ms of
GPU time. How often the real judge is right is Lesson 4's measurement, not
this suite's: on the built pairs, Qwen3.5-9B flagged none of the 40 supported
claims and passed none of the 80 unsupported ones, a clean record whose true
error rate could still be up to about 7%; and on real drafts, it and a second
judge disagreed on 33 of 390 claims. The suite shows where the judge fits in
the loop; Lesson 4 says how far to trust it.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="What the stack costs together" variant="b">

The last row of the first table is the stack's own bill, and it's bigger than
any row above it. All eight layers together wrongly block 2 of the 8 fine
scenarios, a quarter of them, although no single layer blocks more than one.
That's the compounding from
[Lesson 2](/06-reliability/02-reliability-tradeoffs/05-when-checks-add-up/#a-run-has-to-get-past-every-check).
On real traffic, where most runs are fine, small false-block rates in each
layer add up to a noticeable share of users who get an answer withheld or a
question they didn't need.

Latency adds up too, and the counts don't show it. The code layers take
microseconds. The read-back adds a round trip after every write. The support
judge is a model call made after the answer is drafted, so its time is added
to every answer that cites a source. The 12.5 ms above is batched GPU time,
not what a user waits for one call over an API. An approval waits for a
person, which can take minutes or hours. By that measure, the session guard's
one approval is the most expensive layer in the stack, though it's among the
cheapest in compute. A stack's latency has to be measured end to end, in the
[stated unit](/06-reliability/02-reliability-tradeoffs/03-equal-budgets-need-a-stated-unit/)
that matters to the user.

Two things this suite can't show:

- **Layers that miss the same thing.** Each harm here was built for one
  layer, so no two layers share a blind spot.
  [Real checks do](/06-reliability/02-reliability-tradeoffs/05-when-checks-add-up/#checks-that-fail-together),
  and a suite built from real failures would show it.
- **Run-to-run variation.** Every scenario runs once, with a scripted model.
  A real stack needs
  [pass^k](/06-reliability/01-per-step-reliability/03-reliability-as-pass-k/)
  over repeated runs, since a layer that stops a harm on one run may miss it
  on the next.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Breaking the tools on purpose" variant="a">

The suite tests the harms someone thought to build. A third gap is the
failures nobody thought of. Netflix's engineers made a discipline of finding
them. Basiri et al.'s
[*Chaos Engineering*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.05843) (IEEE Software,
2016) describes injecting real-world faults into a running system on purpose,
and checking that it still behaves as it should. The
[principles](https://principlesofchaos.org/) the practice grew into name
malformed responses among the events to vary. For agents, Ruan et al.'s
[ToolEmu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15817) (ICLR 2024) had a language model
play the tools, including their failures. Of the failures it found in
agents, 68.8% were judged ones that would really happen with real tools.

The suite's fine scenarios are ready-made for this. Keep the request and the
model's turns, and break the tools underneath: an empty record, fields that
come back null, JSON cut off halfway, the failure shapes
[Lesson 3](/06-reliability/03-checks-in-the-loop/02-checking-a-tool-result/#what-a-result-check-looks-for)
listed.

<LiveDemo client:load code={FAULT_DEMO} setupCode={SETUP} />

*(Scripted scenarios with faults injected into their read tools. The
scripted model gives its usual answer whatever the tools return, so every
answer that gets through is a confident answer built on a broken result.)*

The faults found two things the suite never showed:

- **A check that crashes.** The result check in this stack was trimmed to
  the one silent failure the suite contains, fields that come back null.
  Given JSON cut off halfway, it raised an exception and took the whole run
  down with it, the thing
  [Lesson 3's check was built never to do](/06-reliability/03-checks-in-the-loop/02-checking-a-tool-result/),
  and a check that fails with
  [no mode chosen](/06-reliability/03-checks-in-the-loop/04-parallel-checks-and-tripwires/#when-the-check-itself-fails).
- **A gap.** An empty record for a lookup got through. Nothing in it was
  null, there was no figure for the grounding check to test, and no error for
  the missing-part check to look for.

Swapping in Lesson 3's fuller result check, which parses carefully and
requires the fields the next step needs, catches every one. That's the loop
fault injection is for: break things on purpose, find what the checks miss,
fix it, and keep the faults in the suite so the fix stays fixed. Running such
a suite on every change to the agent is Module 7's subject.

</Subsection>
