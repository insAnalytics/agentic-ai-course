<Subsection title="Beyond one kind of fact" variant="a">

[Module 5's check for sources that disagree](/05-rag-systems/10-grounded-answers/04-sources-that-disagree/#a-targeted-check-for-facts-that-matter)
looked for one phrase, a number of requests per minute, and flagged the
answer when the sources sent stated different values. It said plainly what
it would miss: "sixty calls a minute", a limit given per hour, and every
kind of disagreement that isn't about a number. Finding contradictions in
general means reading two statements and deciding whether both can be true,
which needs a model, and Module 5 left that to this module.

Deciding whether one statement contradicts another is one of the three
labels of natural language inference, alongside entailment (one supports
the other) and neutral (neither). Research on how well language models do it
over real documents is sobering. Li, Raheja and Kumar's
[ContraDoc](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09182) (NAACL 2024) had people
annotate contradictions in long documents from several domains and tested
GPT-3.5, GPT-4, PaLM 2 and Llama 2 on finding them. GPT-4 did best, and
could outperform people at the task, but the authors still found it
unreliable, and weakest on contradictions that need nuance and context to
see.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Three judges on 120 statement pairs" variant="b">

This lesson's set V holds 120 pairs of statements, built from the same 40
facts as the support pairs. Each fact, as the document states it, is paired
three ways:

- **with its altered version,** one thing changed, which contradicts it
- **with its reworded version,** the same fact in different words, which
  doesn't
- **with another claim from the same document,** a different fact that's
  compatible with it

The reworded and compatible pairs are there to measure false alarms. Here's
what the same three judges said, the model judges asked whether both
statements could be true at once about the same system:

On these pairs, the model judges' failures went one way: they missed
contradictions, 5 for the 9B and 15 for the 4B, and raised no false alarms.
The small inference model did both, missing 8 contradictions and calling 7
compatible pairs contradictory. That's what happened on 120 pairs with these
judges. ContraDoc is the evidence that missed contradictions are a general
weakness; the absence of false alarms here is only this data.

</Subsection>

Here are the 9B's five misses:

*(`statement_pairs` and `judges` from the demo above are already loaded

They're worth reading one by one, because they aren't all the judge's
fault:

- **The first needs a step of reasoning.** A doesn't say standard-tier
  agents can't use `claude-opus`; it says an agent must move to priority
  first. The contradiction follows, but only by inference.
- **The second is a clear miss.** Two definitions of the same alert, with
  different thresholds, can't both be true.
- **The last three hinge on whether the statements are about the same
  thing,** and the label assumed they are. A certificate expiring at 06:00
  doesn't rule out a memory problem at 06:00 unless both are the cause of the
  same incident, which A doesn't say. "A configuration change" in B needn't
  be the one in A. And a failover that *should* take 5 minutes can still
  *normally* take an hour. The judge was asked whether both could be true,
  and read strictly, they could.

That last group is the nuance and context ContraDoc describes. Two
statements only contradict each other if they're about the same thing, and
deciding that is part of the task, for the judge and for whoever writes the
labels. It's also a reminder that built labels are only as good as the
statements they're built from: three of these five are arguably this set's
mistakes, not the model's.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="False alarms on unrelated statements" variant="b">

The small inference model's false alarms look different:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={STATEMENTS_DATA} setupCode={JUDGES_SETUP} code={FALSE_ALARM_DEMO} />

*(`statement_pairs` from the first demo is already loaded here.)*

These are about entirely different things, and the model called them
contradictions with near-certainty. Whatever the cause, the practical
lesson is the same for any judge: only ask it about pairs that are about
the same subject.

That's also what keeps the check affordable. Comparing every statement with
every other grows with the square of their number: 20 statements, say four
from each of five retrieved chunks, make 190 pairs, and each would be a
model call. A cheap test in code for whether two statements share a subject,
such as the same agent, error code or setting, cuts that to the few pairs
where a contradiction is possible. That's the exercise below.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="When a contradiction is found" variant="a">

Finding the conflict is half the job. What to do with it is what
[Module 5 set up](/05-rag-systems/10-grounded-answers/04-sources-that-disagree/#giving-the-model-what-it-needs-to-choose):
give the model each source's date and type, prefer the newer official
source, and say in the answer that the sources disagree. The general check
adds one thing: it can flag the conflict whatever the model then writes, so
an answer that quietly follows the older source can be caught, and so can the
documents, which someone should reconcile.

</Subsection>
