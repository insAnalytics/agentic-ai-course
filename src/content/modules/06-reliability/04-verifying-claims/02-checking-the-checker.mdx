<Subsection title="A check you haven't measured is a guess" variant="a">

The support check from
[the previous concept](/06-reliability/04-verifying-claims/01-checking-each-claim/)
is itself a model, and it can be wrong in both directions: passing a claim
the source doesn't support, or flagging one it does. Shipping it without
knowing how often each happens would undo the point of
[Lesson 2's habit](/06-reliability/02-reliability-tradeoffs/01-four-things-every-technique-trades/#a-refusal-can-be-a-reliability-failure):
a check is known by what it catches and what it wrongly blocks.

Measuring a model-based judge against cases with known answers is standard
practice. Zheng et al.'s
[study of LLM judges](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685) (NeurIPS 2023),
which made the approach common, validated their judges by how often they
agreed with human ratings. Evaluation guides from tooling vendors such as
[Arize](https://arize.com/guides/llm-as-a-judge/) give the same advice: run
the judge on a labelled "golden" set, compare its labels with yours, and
only rely on it once they agree.

The research also says what to expect. Li et al.'s
[AttributionBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15089) (ACL Findings 2024)
collected existing datasets of claims labelled as supported or not by their
evidence, and found the task hard: even a fine-tuned GPT-3.5 reached only
about 80% macro-F1, a score that weighs catching unsupported claims and
passing supported ones equally. Difficulty depended on length. Zero-shot,
GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 scored over 80% on datasets with short claims and evidence,
and around 60–70% where the evidence was long. Models trained for natural
language inference, the task of deciding whether one text supports or
contradicts another, often did as well as the fine-tuned GPT-3.5.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Labels without a labelling team" variant="b">

A golden set usually means people labelling examples. This lesson's set
takes a shortcut that works for this one question. For each of 40 facts
from the course's documents, it pairs three claims with sources, and the
right answer follows from how each pair was made:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={SET_DATA} setupCode={LOAD_VERIFICATION} code={PAIRS_DEMO} />

*(One of the 40 facts in set V.)*

- **Restated:** the fact in new words, with the chunk it came from. The
  source supports it.
- **Altered:** the fact with one thing changed, with the same chunk. The
  source contradicts it.
- **Other source:** the restated claim with a chunk from another document
  that doesn't state it. The source is silent on it.

One of these needed a person. The corpus repeats facts across documents, so
the best-matching chunk from another document often did state the claim.
Each "other source" was chosen by reading candidates in full and picking one
that doesn't.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="How the three judges did" variant="a">

The same 120 pairs went to three judges: Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.5-4B with
the prompt from the previous concept, and `cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base`,
a small natural language inference (NLI) model that scores a pair as
entailment, contradiction or neutral. It counts as saying "supported" when entailment
is its most likely label.

*(Real verdicts from the committed runs: the model judges greedy, one call
per pair.)*

- **The 9B agreed with every label.**
- **The 4B flagged 3 claims its source supports,** and passed nothing it
  shouldn't have.
- **The NLI model flagged 4 supported claims and passed 1 altered one.**

On these pairs, then, the model judges' mistakes were all false flags: they
passed nothing unsupported. That's what happened on 120 pairs with these
two models, not a general property of model judges.

The NLI model's "supported" comes from a probability, so where to draw the
line is a choice. Here's what moving it does:

*(Real scores from the committed run.)*

Raising the bar only adds false flags until 0.97. Only there does it catch the

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Zero errors isn't a zero error rate" variant="b">

The 9B's perfect score doesn't mean it never errs. A judge that is wrong 5%
of the time will still get 40 cases in a row right about one time in eight.
The standard way to read a clean record is Hanley and Lippman-Hand's
[rule of three](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/c607/ch08/zero_numerator.pdf)
(JAMA, 1983): after zero failures in n independent tries, the true failure
rate could still be as high as about 3/n, at 95% confidence. Here's the
exact version:

<LiveDemo client:load code={BOUND_DEMO} />

The third line matters. The 80 unsupported pairs came from only 40 facts,
and pairs built from the same fact aren't independent tries: a judge that
misreads a fact may misread both its pairs. Counting facts instead of pairs
is the same correction
[Lesson 1 made](/06-reliability/01-per-step-reliability/02-the-same-question-run-twice/#how-sure-can-we-be-of-these-numbers)
when it resampled whole questions. With 40 facts, the honest reading of the
9B's result is "wrong less than about 7% of the time, on pairs like these".

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Why this is the easy end" variant="a">

"Pairs like these" is doing a lot of work. Each built pair is one short
claim against one chunk, and each unsupported claim differs from the truth
in one clear way, or sits beside an unrelated chunk. That's the easy end of
what AttributionBench measured.

Real answers are harder, and the previous concept already showed it. On the
4B's own drafts, the 9B and 4B judges disagreed on 33 of 390 cited claims.
On each of those, at least one judge is wrong, so on real drafts at least
one of them erred on at least 33 claims, about 8.5%, where on the built pairs
the two made 3 mistakes between them, across 120 pairs each. The drafts have
no labels, so that's a floor on the errors, not a measurement of them.

The built set is still worth having: a judge that fails it is certainly not
ready. But a judge that passes it has only cleared the first bar. Checking
it on a sample of real outputs, labelled by a person, is the step after.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Judges have habits" variant="b">

Beyond random mistakes, model judges have systematic ones.
[Module 5 met three of them](/05-rag-systems/02-measuring-retrieval/05-when-the-labels-are-wrong/#a-model-as-the-judge)
while using a model to label passages: leniency, being swayed by order and
length, and favouring work from their own model. The research puts sizes on
them, and adds one more:

- **Order and length.** Zheng et al., in
  [the study above](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685), which is the one
  Module 5 cited, measured both. Swapping the order of two answers changed
  a judge's verdict often. GPT-4, the most
  consistent, gave the same verdict both ways about 65% of the time. Padding
  an answer with repeated content fooled Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 in about 91%
  of their test cases, and GPT-4 in about 9%.
- **Their own output.** This is the *self-preference bias*
  [Module 2 named](/02-the-agent-loop/09-reflection-and-self-critique/03-the-limits-shared-blind-spots/#reflection-doesn-t-bring-in-a-second-independent-perspective)
  as a limit of reflection. Panickssery, Bowman and Feng,
  [*LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)
  (NeurIPS 2024), found judges scoring their own outputs above others' that
  people rated as equal. The better a model was at recognising its own text,
  the stronger the preference.
- **Models like themselves.** Kim et al.'s
  [study of correlated errors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962) (ICML 2025)
  found judges overrating less accurate models, especially ones from the
  judge's own provider, and underrating models more accurate than themselves.

Practitioners draw the same conclusion from the other side. Hamel Husain's
[guide to LLM judges](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/), written from
work with over 30 companies, has a domain expert give pass/fail verdicts with
a written critique, and revises the judge's prompt until it agrees with the
expert. Anthropic's
[guide to evaluating agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
says model graders "should be closely calibrated with human experts".

This lesson's own setup should be a warning sign: in
[the previous concept](/06-reliability/04-verifying-claims/01-checking-each-claim/),
the 4B judged claims from drafts the 4B wrote. If it favoured its own output,
it would pass its claims where the 9B flagged them:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={SELF_DATA} setupCode={SELF_SETUP} code={SELF_DEMO} />

*(Real verdicts from the committed runs. `claim_outcomes` is the previous
concept's, unchanged.)*

It went the other way. Where the two disagreed, the 4B mostly flagged its own
claims, the same stricter lean it showed on the built pairs. So these runs
show no self-preference, but that's a narrow result. A support check asks a
closed question about one claim and one source, while the research found
self-preference in open-ended judgements of quality. A second model family
as the judge costs little and removes the question. Either way, the judge has
to be measured, not assumed.

</Subsection>
