<Subsection title="The answer that shouldn't be given" variant="a">

Some questions have no answer in the sources: the corpus never recorded
it, or retrieval didn't find it. A model asked anyway will usually still
produce an answer, from its general knowledge or by filling the gap with
something plausible, as
[Module 1's lesson on hallucination](/01-llm-foundations/04-how-llms-generate-text/04-hallucination-as-a-direct-consequence/#not-a-malfunction-the-same-mechanism-landing-on-a-wrong-answer)
explained. In a system built to answer from sources, that answer is worse
than none: it looks as sourced as the rest, and nothing in it is.

So the system needs to be able to say "the sources don't say", and there are
two places to decide it: in code, before the model is called, and in the
model's own reply. This concept looks at both.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="A signal before the model is called" variant="b">

The reranker scores how well each candidate answers the question, so its
best score is a natural measure of whether retrieval found anything
answer-like. Here are those top scores for every labelled question, grouped
by what retrieval actually achieved. This runs every labelled question
through retrieval, so allow several seconds:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={RAG_EXPANSION_DATA} setupCode={SETUP} code={SCORES_DEMO} />

The groups overlap, but they're not the same. Questions whose answer was
retrieved mostly score high, with a median of 6.00. Questions with no answer
in the corpus sit lower, and so do the ones retrieval missed. The overlap is
the problem: four of the ten lowest scores belong to questions whose answer
*was* retrieved.

Two of those four, q24 and q25, are follow-up questions searched exactly as
asked, which Lesson 8 showed should be rewritten first. That's a reminder
that a threshold measures the whole pipeline in front of it: change the
retrieval, and the right threshold changes too.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Choosing a threshold" variant="a">

Abstaining below a threshold trades one kind of mistake for another:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={RAG_EXPANSION_DATA} setupCode={SETUP} code={THRESHOLD_DEMO} />

- **At 0,** the system declines 2 of the 3 questions with no answer in the
  corpus, and 2 of the 7 it would have got wrong anyway, at the cost of 3
  questions it could have answered.
- **Lower,** fewer good answers are lost, but fewer bad ones are caught.
- **Higher,** it starts refusing answerable questions faster than it catches
  anything new.

There's no right threshold in general, only a right one for what a mistake
costs. An internal help tool, where a wrong answer wastes someone's
afternoon, can afford to decline more often. A tool that acts on its answers
should decline more readily still. The labelled set is what lets you see the
trade in numbers rather than guess at it.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="A threshold is a setting to confirm" variant="b">

Those numbers flatter the threshold a little. It was chosen by looking at
these 46 questions, and every setting chosen on a set of questions fits them
a little, which is why
[Lesson 2 held questions out](/05-rag-systems/02-measuring-retrieval/04-reading-the-numbers-honestly/#the-held-out-set).
And only 3 of the 46 have no answer in the corpus, so "declines 2 of 3"
rests on three examples: one more or one fewer caught would move it by a
third.

So a threshold chosen on the main questions is a setting to confirm on
questions it wasn't chosen on. Here it is, unchanged, on the 11 held-out
questions as well. Nothing is chosen from them: the threshold stays at 0
whatever they show, which keeps Lesson 2's rule. This also runs every
labelled question through retrieval:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={RAG_EXPANSION_DATA} setupCode={SETUP} code={HELD_OUT_DEMO} />

On the held-out questions, the threshold declines the one question with no
answer in the corpus, and loses 2 of the 9 whose answer was retrieved,
against 3 of 36 on the main set. One of those two, h11, is another follow-up
question searched exactly as asked, like q24 and q25. With so few questions,
neither difference means much. One caught out of one isn't evidence that the
threshold does better on new questions, and 2 of 9 against 3 of 36 isn't
firm evidence that it does worse. Across both sets there are only four
questions with no answer, far too few to put a rate on. What a team would do
is what Lesson 2 did for retrieval: collect many more questions the corpus
can't answer, from real traffic where possible, label them, and keep some
aside to check the threshold on.

The number itself belongs to this reranker. As
[Lesson 7 noted](/05-rag-systems/07-reranking/01-two-stages/#scores-computed-ahead-of-time),
its scores are raw numbers, not probabilities. On the main questions the top
scores run from -5.58 to 9.57, and 0 was chosen because that's where these
questions split, not because it means anything in itself. Another reranker
scores on its own scale, and even this one gives different scores to
different text: the scores here are for chunks with Lesson 9's contexts at
the top, not the plain chunks Lesson 7 scored. Change the reranker or what
it reads, and the threshold has to be chosen and confirmed again.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="What a threshold can't see" variant="a">

The highest-scoring question with no answer in the corpus is "Which on-call
engineer handled INC-2093?", at 4.93, well above any sensible threshold. A
score can't catch it, so the model has to: the instructions ask it to say
"the sources don't say" rather than guess, and code needs to recognise when
it has. This is already loaded for every demo and exercise from here to the
end of this lesson:

```python
DECLINE = "the sources don't say"

def declined(answer: str) -> bool:
    """Whether the model declined, recognised by the phrase the instructions ask it to use."""
    return DECLINE in answer.lower().replace("\u2019", "'")
```

Here's the question, with a scripted reply:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={RAG_EXPANSION_DATA} setupCode={SETUP_WITH_CLIENT} code={DECLINE_DEMO} />

*The model's reply is scripted to show the mechanics.*

Retrieval did its job. It found exactly the right document, the incident's
timeline, which is why the score is high. The specific fact asked for just
isn't in it, and only something that reads the sources against the question
can notice that.

A decline is an answer, not a failure, so it shouldn't be treated like an
ordinary statement. Here `check_citations` flags it as uncited, which is
technically true and practically wrong. Checking `declined` first lets the
answer step handle declines separately.

Recognising a decline by a fixed phrase is fragile: a model that says "the
documents don't mention" instead slips through. A sturdier design asks for
[structured output](/01-llm-foundations/10-structured-output-and-tool-calling/02-constrained-decoding/#a-hard-guarantee-not-a-soft-nudge)
with an explicit field, such as `"answerable": false`, so the decision is data
rather than wording. The phrase check keeps this lesson's code short; the
principle is the same.

Some replies are neither an answer nor a decline. Asked a two-part question
whose sources cover only one part, a good reply answers that part, with its
citation, and says the sources don't say the rest. `declined` finds the
phrase and calls the whole reply a decline, so the part that was answered
would be handled as a decline too: a status that's either answered or
declined has no way to say "partly". Structured output can, with a field for
each part of the question.

</Subsection>
