<Subsection title="Building the graph" variant="a">

A graph index is paid for up front, and the biggest part is extraction: one
model call per chunk. Here's what this lesson's extraction cost, and what the
same prompt would cost over the whole corpus, in tokens:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={RAG_GRAPH_DATA} setupCode={SETUP} code={COST_DEMO} />

Extracting from every chunk would read about 680,000 input tokens, nearly
three times the corpus itself, because each call carries the extraction prompt
and the chunk's title and section as well as the chunk. The output estimate is
rough: it assumes vendor documentation yields relationships at the same rate
as incident reports, which it probably doesn't. Output tokens also usually
cost several times more per token than input.

That's still far less than Lesson 9's contextual retrieval, whose prompts
carried the whole document for every chunk. Extraction needs only the chunk,
its title and its section. But extraction isn't the whole bill:

- **Entity resolution,** done here by hand, is either a person's time or
  more model calls and a review.
- **Community summaries,** one model call per community, and in the paper's
  design, for every level of a hierarchy of communities.
- **Global questions** then cost a model call per community used, every time.
- **Keeping it current.** Edit one incident report, and its chunks need
  extracting again, the new names resolving against the old, the communities
  recomputing, and every summary touching them rewriting. Microsoft's GraphRAG
  library can [update an existing index](https://microsoft.github.io/graphrag/cli/)
  instead of rebuilding it: new documents are extracted on their own and
  merged in, and it tries to avoid recomputing communities, though sometimes
  it has to. Keeping indexes in
  step with changing documents is Module 10's subject; a graph is one of the
  more expensive indexes to keep in step.

  One detail matters from the first build. This lesson files each chunk's
  triples, and so every edge's sources, under ids like `D11:1`: the chunk's
  place in its document. An edit that adds a paragraph near the top renumbers
  every chunk after it, and an edge extracted before the edit then cites a
  different passage, with nothing to say so. Filing extractions under a hash
  of the chunk's text instead, the way
  [Lesson 4 files vectors](/05-rag-systems/04-search-by-meaning/01-from-similarity-to-search/#vectors-computed-ahead-of-time),
  turns that silent mismatch into a visible gap: a changed chunk has a new
  hash, finds no stored triples, and is extracted again.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="What Microsoft has published since" variant="b">

The cost of building summaries up front was the approach's best-known
drawback, and Microsoft's later work attacks it directly.
**[LazyGraphRAG](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag-setting-a-new-standard-for-quality-and-cost/)**,
from Microsoft Research, drops both expensive steps from indexing. It finds
concepts with ordinary language processing, extracting noun phrases and
noting which appear together, instead of asking a model, and it builds no
summaries in advance, deferring model calls to query time. They report that
its indexing costs the same as vector RAG, and 0.1% of full GraphRAG. On global questions, they report answer quality
comparable to GraphRAG's global search at more than 700 times lower query
cost, on their own benchmark of news articles, scored by a model comparing
answers.

The gains themselves have been questioned too.
[Zeng and colleagues (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06331) argued that the
standard evaluations of GraphRAG methods had two flaws: questions not closely
related to the data, and biases in how a model judges competing answers. With
both addressed, they found the performance gains of three GraphRAG methods
much more moderate than previously reported. That doesn't make the approach
wrong. It means its advantages, like everything in this module, need
measuring on your own questions.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Graphs and vectors together" variant="a">

This lesson used its graph in two ways: walking fixed edges for chains, and
summarising communities for global questions. Published systems also mix a
graph with vector search in other ways. Here's a short map of some that are
often compared, not a guide to any of them:

- **GraphRAG's local search**
  ([Microsoft](https://microsoft.github.io/graphrag/query/local_search/)) is
  entity-centred. It finds the entities most related to the question by
  their embedded descriptions, as
  [the traversal concept's linker](/05-rag-systems/12-graph-rag/03-answering-by-traversal/#finding-where-to-start)
  would at scale, then puts their neighbours, relationships, source text and
  community reports into one context. It answers questions about particular
  things, where global search answers questions about everything.
- **DRIFT search**
  ([Microsoft Research, 2024](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/introducing-drift-search-combining-global-and-local-search-methods-to-improve-quality-and-efficiency/))
  combines the two. It compares the question with the most relevant
  community reports, drafts a broad first answer with follow-up questions,
  and answers those with local search.
- **LightRAG** ([Guo and colleagues, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05779))
  extracts a graph as GraphRAG does, but finds no communities and writes no
  summaries. The model pulls specific and broad keywords from the question;
  vector search matches the specific ones to entities and the broad ones to
  relationships, and the graph adds their neighbours.
- **HippoRAG** ([Gutiérrez and colleagues, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14831))
  builds a graph from triples extracted from every passage, with no fixed
  list of relations. The question's entities become the starting points of
  **Personalized PageRank**, which spreads a score outwards along the edges,
  and passages are ranked by the score their entities collect.
  **HippoRAG 2** ([2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14802)) adds the passages
  themselves to the graph as nodes, and picks the starting points by matching
  the whole question against the graph's triples.

They share a shape: vectors find where to enter the graph, and the graph
reaches what the vectors alone wouldn't. They differ in what happens next:
gathering neighbours, following up with more questions, or spreading a
score. DRIFT, LightRAG and both HippoRAGs each report gains on their
authors' own evaluations, and Zeng and colleagues' caution about how such
gains are measured applies here too.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="When it's worth it" variant="b">

A graph earns its cost when questions are about **structure**, and the
structure is only written down in prose:

- **Chains of relationships:** dependencies between services, who reports to
  whom, which supplier provides which part. This lesson's traversal answered
  four relationship questions from their cited chunks, where the pipeline
  missed the one that needed a chain.
- **Questions about the whole collection:** themes, recurring causes,
  patterns across many reports, where any top k is only a sample.

It's not worth it when:

- **The relationships already exist as data.** If a service catalogue,
  a configuration system or a database records which service calls which, build
  the graph from that, or query it with a tool, as Lesson 11 queried the
  registry. Extracting from prose what a system of record already holds adds
  cost and errors.
- **Questions are mostly about single facts.** Most of this module's
  labelled questions are, and hybrid search with reranking answers them for
  far less.
- **An agent's searches can follow the chain.** Lesson 11's multi-hop search
  handled two-link chains with a few searches and no index to build. A graph
  pays off when chains are long, or when the same structure is asked about
  again and again.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="A graph database as an agent's tool" variant="a">

When the relationships live in a system of record, such as a service
catalogue or a configuration management database (CMDB), there's nothing to
extract. The catalogue already says which service calls which, and it's kept
current by the people who change the services. Load those relationships into
a **graph database**, which stores nodes and edges and follows them
efficiently, and give the agent a tool that queries it. It's the graph
counterpart of
[Lesson 11's SQL tool](/05-rag-systems/11-agentic-rag/04-when-the-answer-is-in-a-table/#guardrails-allow-don-t-ban).

Graph databases have their own query languages. The best known is
**Cypher**, from Neo4j, which other databases implement as openCypher, and
on which the ISO's graph query standard, GQL, published in 2024, is largely
based. A chain is one pattern in it: roughly, "match every agent with a path
of up to five dependency edges to auth-service". A graph tool can take two
shapes:

- **A fixed query, with the model supplying a name,** like `find_dependents`,
  the graph tool at the end of this lesson. You write the query; the model can only choose where it
  starts. It answers fewer kinds of question, and there's little to guard
  beyond the name.
- **The model writes the query,** called text-to-Cypher, the graph version of
  text-to-SQL. It can answer questions nobody wrote a query for, and it needs
  every guardrail Lesson 11 built: a **read-only** account; an **allowlist**
  of the node labels and relationship types the tool can reach (Neo4j, for
  one, can grant a role access to particular labels and relationship types);
  a **row limit**; and a **timeout**, with a cap on path length, since a path
  pattern with no upper bound can explore much of the graph, the graph's
  version of a runaway join.

Either way, the answer cites the system of record, as Lesson 11's answers
cited the registry: a graph built from data has no source chunks, but it
still has a source.

</Subsection>
