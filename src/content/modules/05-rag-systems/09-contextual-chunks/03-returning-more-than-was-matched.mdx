<Subsection title="Small to find, big to read" variant="a">

The previous two concepts added context to each chunk *before* searching.
There's a second family of fixes that works *after*: search with small,
precise chunks, which match a question sharply, then hand the model
something bigger around each match. It goes by names like small-to-big or
parent-document retrieval, and the common frameworks build in both of its
forms: LlamaIndex's
[sentence windows](https://developers.llamaindex.ai/python/framework/module_guides/loading/node_parsers/modules/)
for the first, and LangChain's
[`ParentDocumentRetriever`](https://python.langchain.com/docs/how_to/parent_document_retriever/)
for the second:

- **Neighbours.** Return each matched chunk with the chunks just before and
  after it in its document. This is what Lesson 3's chunk numbers were
  for: `(doc_id, chunk)` names a position, so the chunks either side are one
  lookup away.
- **Parents.** Return the whole section a matched chunk belongs to.

Here's the kind of case it's meant for, with 100-token chunks. An asterisk
marks a chunk that answers the question:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={RAG_EXPANSION_DATA} setupCode={SETUP} code={NEIGHBOUR_DEMO} />

The best match is step 1 of the key-rotation runbook, about issuing a new
key. The answer, that an exposed key is rotated first and investigated
second, is in the chunk just before it. Returning the neighbours would have
included it.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="At an equal budget" variant="b">

Returning more text always finds more answers, if cost is ignored, so the
only fair comparison is the one Lesson 3 set up: the same token budget for
every approach. Here are the module's 200-token chunks, 100-token chunks,
and 100-token chunks expanded to their whole sections, through the hybrid
first stage. It builds two first stages over the whole corpus, so allow
twenty seconds or so:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={RAG_EXPANSION_DATA} setupCode={SETUP} code={PARENTS_DEMO} />

Small chunks alone do exactly as well as 200-token chunks at every budget,
and expanding them to whole sections does *worse*. The reason is the budget
itself. Every token spent on a matched chunk's surroundings is a token not
spent on the next-best match, and the next match is often worth more. The
structured chunker also already keeps sections together wherever it can, so
a "parent" is often much of what a 200-token chunk already was. Whether
neighbours, a smaller expansion than whole sections, do any better is this
concept's exercise.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Fixed windows, and a caution about our own measure" variant="a">

One more comparison, because it tests this whole lesson's premise. Fixed
windows, Lesson 3's blind cut every 200 tokens, ignore headings, so the
first window of each document contains its title and its first section
together. That's a crude, automatic form of context. Here they are against
the structured chunks with and without the model-written contexts, counted
two ways. This one builds three first stages, so again allow ten seconds or
more:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={RAG_EXPANSION_DATA} setupCode={SETUP} code={WINDOWS_DEMO} />

Counted with Lesson 2's rule, where a chunk holding at least half of a quote
counts as relevant, fixed windows come out on top. But that rule was
designed to survive re-chunking, and it's lenient in exactly the way fixed
windows exploit: a window that cut a sentence in half still holds half of
it. Counted strictly, with the whole quote in one chunk, their lead mostly
disappears: one question ahead at 1,000 tokens, level at 3,000. Structured
chunks with contexts get there without cutting a sentence.

Two things to take from that. First, the context fixed windows carry by
accident is real, and it's the same thing headers and contexts supply on
purpose. Second, every measure has a bias, and a technique can win by
fitting the measure rather than the need. Checking the result with a
stricter count is how you tell the difference.

</Subsection>
