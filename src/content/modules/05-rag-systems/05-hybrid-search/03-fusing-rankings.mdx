def rrf(rankings: list[list[dict]], k: int = 60) -> list[dict]:
    """Reciprocal Rank Fusion: each chunk scores the sum of 1 / (k + rank) over the rankings it appears in."""
    scores, found = defaultdict(float), {}

<Subsection title="Two rankings, two scales" variant="a">

BM25 and search by meaning fail differently. Search by meaning lost
"MON-2002", a bare code with no meaning to place; keyword search finds exact
strings but misses paraphrases. The obvious move is to run both and combine
their results. The obvious way to combine them, adding the scores, doesn't
work:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={RAG_BGE_DATA} setupCode={SETUP} code={SCALES_DEMO} />

The two searches score on different scales. BM25's scores are sums of
weights with no fixed upper limit; they depend on how many rare words match
and how long the chunk is. Cosine similarities sit in a narrow band below 1.
Add them, and BM25 decides almost everything. Rescaling each list to the
range 0 to 1 helps less than it seems: it depends on each question's spread
of scores, and one unusually high score squashes all the others.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Reciprocal Rank Fusion" variant="b">

**Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)**, from
[Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1571941.1572114),
sidesteps the scales by ignoring scores altogether and using only ranks.
Each chunk gets:

> RRF score = Σ over the rankings it appears in of 1 / (k + rank)

A chunk ranked first in a list earns 1/(k + 1), second earns 1/(k + 2), and
so on; a chunk missing from a list earns nothing from it. Chunks near the top
of both lists collect two large shares and rise; a chunk near the top of one
list can still do well. Here it is on the question search by meaning lost:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={RAG_BGE_DATA} setupCode={SETUP} code={MON_DEMO} />

Only two chunks contain `MON-2002`, and BM25 ranks the error table first.
Search by meaning puts it 16th, behind pages that merely look like
monitoring. Fusion puts it first, because a first place in one list plus a
16th in the other beats a first place in one list alone.

The constant k controls how much a single list's top places count. With
k = 60, first place in one list is worth 1/61, about 0.016, and first place
in both is worth twice that. A small k makes the top of any one list
dominate; a larger k flattens the differences between ranks. In the paper's
words, the constant "mitigates the impact of high rankings by outlier
systems."

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Why k = 60, and how much it matters" variant="a">

The value comes from the paper's pilot experiments, and it was then fixed
and never tuned again: k = 60 was set in a pilot study and not altered in
later validation. The pilot found it near-optimal but not critical. On its
first test collection, mean average precision was 0.2072 at k = 0, 0.2134
at k = 20, 0.2145 at k = 60, 0.2142 at k = 100 and 0.2098 at k = 500: a broad
plateau. In their pilot and TREC experiments, RRF beat two established fusion
methods, Condorcet and CombMNZ, and the best individual system, by 4% to 5%
on average.

On this corpus, fusing BM25 with search by meaning, the plateau shows up
too:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={RAG_BGE_DATA} setupCode={SETUP} code={K_DEMO} />

One question separates every value of k tried. RRF's appeal is exactly
this: nothing to tune, no scores to calibrate, and it works for any number
of rankings, which is why it's widely used to combine keyword and vector
results. Whether the combination is *better* than either search alone on
this corpus is a separate question, and the next concept answers it.

</Subsection>
