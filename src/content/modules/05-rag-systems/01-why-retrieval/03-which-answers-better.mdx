<Subsection title="Each approach has its own way to fail" variant="a">

The last concept compared cost. Cost only matters if the answers are good,
so this one asks which approach answers better. Each fails in its own way.

**Sending everything** puts the answer in the context, but the model has to
find it among a quarter of a million tokens of text that doesn't answer the
question. Two effects the course has already met make that harder:

- **Longer input degrades answers.** Module 4 covered
  [the evidence that models get less reliable as input grows](/04-context-and-memory/02-context-that-fits-but-still-hurts/01-agents-get-worse-before-the-window-is-full/#what-the-evidence-shows),
  well inside the window, and worst when the extra text resembles what the
  model is looking for. A knowledge base is full of exactly that: pages
  about alerts, keys and failovers that look like the one page that
  answers.
- **Position matters.** Models use information at the start and end of
  their input more reliably than
  [information in the middle](/01-llm-foundations/06-context-windows-and-kv-cache/05-uneven-use-of-long-contexts/#the-lost-in-the-middle-effect).
  In a 232,000-token corpus, almost every passage is in the middle.

**Sending what was retrieved** gives the model a short, focused context,
but only if the search found the right passages. If it didn't, the model
never sees the answer, and nothing after the search can fix that. The
model will either say it doesn't know or, worse, answer from its own
guesses.

Which failure is more common is an empirical question, and two studies
have measured it directly.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="What the head-to-head studies found" variant="b">

**Google DeepMind, 2024.** Zhuowan Li and colleagues compared the two approaches
on nine question-answering datasets with three models: Gemini-1.5-Pro,
GPT-4o and GPT-3.5-Turbo. Their retrieval was a basic setup: documents cut
into 300-word chunks, and the five most similar chunks to the question
sent to the model. What they found:

- **Sending everything scored higher on average, for all three models.**
  On their main results table, the average scores for everything against
  retrieval were 49.7 against 37.3 for Gemini-1.5-Pro, 48.7 against 32.6
  for GPT-4o, and 32.1 against 30.3 for GPT-3.5-Turbo.
- **Except when the text was far larger than the window.** On two datasets
  averaging about 147,000 words, GPT-3.5-Turbo, with a 16,000-token window,
  did better with retrieval.
- **Most of the time, both approaches gave the same answer.** For 63% of
  questions, the two predictions were identical, right or wrong.
- **Retrieval's failures had recognisable causes.** Examining the
  questions retrieval couldn't answer, the authors found four kinds:
  questions needing several steps, where one answer is needed to search
  for the next; general questions that are hard to turn into a search;
  long, complex questions; and implicit questions that depend on
  understanding the whole text.

**LaRA, 2025.** Kuan Li and colleagues, at HKUST, Alibaba's Tongyi Lab and
Penn State, built a benchmark to
fix flaws they found in earlier comparisons, such as texts too short to
matter and datasets the models had likely seen in training. It has 2,326
questions over novels, financial reports and academic papers, at two
lengths, about 32,000 and 128,000 tokens, tested on eleven models. Their
retrieval was stronger than a basic setup, combining keyword and
similarity search. What they found:

- **The strongest models did better with everything, at both lengths.**
  GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet and Gemini-1.5-Pro all favoured sending the
  whole text.
- **Across all eleven models, the balance shifted with length.** At 32,000
  tokens, sending everything averaged 2.4 points higher; at 128,000,
  retrieval averaged 3.68 points higher. Weaker models gained most from
  retrieval.
- **Comparison questions were retrieval's weakest task.** Questions that
  need two separate parts of a text trailed by about 15 points, because
  the search had to find both parts.
- **Retrieval was better at saying "that isn't in the text".** On
  questions whose answer wasn't in the document, retrieval did better, by
  10.4 points at 32,000 tokens and 22.4 at 128,000. With the whole text in
  front of them, models more often invented an answer.
- **Only the full-text approach showed lost in the middle.** Its accuracy
  dropped when the answer sat near the centre; retrieval's didn't depend
  on where the answer was.

*Sources: Li et al., "[Retrieval Augmented Generation or Long-Context LLMs?
A Comprehensive Study and Hybrid Approach](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16833)"
(EMNLP 2024); Li et al., "[LaRA: Benchmarking Retrieval-Augmented Generation
and Long-Context LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09977)" (2025).*

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Reading the evidence honestly" variant="a">

Three conclusions hold up, and one caution comes with them.

**When the corpus fits and the model is strong, sending everything is
often as good or better.** Both studies agree on that. It's the honest
answer to "is retrieval better?": not automatically, and not for quality
alone.

**Retrieval is only as good as its search.** The DeepMind team showed this
with a test where one sentence, "the passkey is 123456", is hidden in
irrelevant text. Asked "What is the passkey?", retrieval scored 80.3%
against 65.3% for the whole text. Reworded as "What is the special token
hidden inside the texts?", retrieval fell to 4.6% while the whole text held
at 69.3%. Nothing about the text changed, only the words of the question.
That's why the next lesson measures retrieval before this module improves
anything.

**The failures point to fixes, and most of this module is those fixes.**

- Questions needing several steps, and the comparison questions LaRA
  found hard, are what Lesson 11's agentic retrieval is for: an agent
  that searches, reads, and searches again.
- Questions about the corpus as a whole are the kind Lesson 12's graph
  approach answers.
- Complex or oddly worded questions are what Lesson 8's query rewriting
  works on.
- Saying "that isn't in the text", where retrieval already does better, is
  built on deliberately in Lesson 10.

**The caution:** both studies tested 2024 models. Long-context ability has
improved since, so the size of every gap above is out of date, even if the
pattern holds. Treat the numbers as evidence that the choice depends on the
model, the task and the corpus, not as figures to plan with. The way to
know for your corpus is to measure on your corpus.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="The reasons that don't depend on the benchmark" variant="b">

Quality is contested. Four other reasons for retrieval aren't:

- **Cost and latency,** from
  [the last concept](/05-rag-systems/01-why-retrieval/02-what-pasting-everything-costs/#putting-a-number-on-it):
  far fewer tokens per question, or per turn in an agent.
- **Scale:** past the window, sending everything isn't an option at all.
- **Freshness:** a new document is searchable as soon as it's indexed,
  without re-sending or re-caching the whole corpus.
- **Permissions:** a search can leave out documents the user may not read.
  Sending everything can't, without building a separate "everything" for
  each set of permissions.

And the two approaches combine. The DeepMind team's **Self-Route** tries
retrieval first and asks the model whether it can answer from what was
retrieved. Only when it says it can't does the full text get sent. With
Gemini-1.5-Pro, the retrieval step answered 82% of questions, cutting
cost by 65% with scores close to sending everything every time. Deciding,
question by question, whether retrieved text is enough is a decision an
agent can make, and Lesson 11 returns to it.

</Subsection>
