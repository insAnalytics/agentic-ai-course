<Subsection title="Measured against what?" variant="a">

[Lesson 6 ended](/07-evaluation/06-model-graders/06-recap-practice/) with
judges whose verdicts disagree with each other, follow their rubrics into
wrong answers, and never say they're unsure.
[Anthropic's guide to agent evals](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
is direct about what that means: model graders need calibrating against
human graders before their numbers are trusted, and calibrating them is
one of the two things it says to reserve systematic human grading for. A
judge is a measuring instrument, and an instrument is checked against a
reference it's meant to reproduce. For a grader, the reference is a
careful person answering the same question.

This lesson does that check. Its main material is labelling done for the
purpose, which comes in the next concepts. This concept sets out how to
compare a grader with a person, using labels the module already has:
[Lesson 3's reading](/07-evaluation/03-error-analysis/01-from-a-fixed-map/#the-sample)
of 50 runs of Module 5's questions, each given a pass or fail verdict, set
beside
[the correctness judges'](/07-evaluation/06-model-graders/04-correctness-and-relevance/#the-answers-judged)
verdicts on the same runs.

That comparison isn't quite fair to the judges, and it's worth saying why
before using it. The reading judged each run as a whole, by the written
standard, which fails a wrong citation; the correctness judge was asked
only whether the answer matches the reference. Where they differ, the
judge may simply have answered its own question correctly. The labels in
the rest of the lesson are given on the judge's own question, blind,
which removes that problem.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Why accuracy isn't enough" variant="b">

The obvious summary is accuracy, the share of runs where the grader and
the person agree. It hides the failure that matters most:

```python
import json
from pathlib import Path

JUDGE_LABELS = Path("/data/eval/judge-labels")


def load_vs_reading() -> list[dict]:
    """The 50 question runs Lesson 3's reading labelled, with the reading's verdict, both judges' correctness
    verdicts, and whether every citation was to a source a tool returned."""
    return json.loads((JUDGE_LABELS / "vs-reading.json").read_text(encoding="utf-8"))["rows"]
```
*(defined once here and already loaded for every demo in this lesson)*

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={VS_READING_DATA} setupCode={LOAD_JUDGE_LABELS} code={DO_NOTHING_DEMO} />

*(The reading's verdicts are Lesson 3's labels, Simar's where he read the
run and Claude's otherwise.)*

A grader that does nothing scores 70%, because most runs pass. The higher
the pass rate, the better doing nothing looks, and an agent improving makes
a useless grader look better.
[Husain and Shankar's method for validating a judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)
reports two numbers instead:

- **TPR (true positive rate):** of the runs the person passed, the share
  the grader passed too.
- **TNR (true negative rate):** of the runs the person failed, the share
  the grader failed too.

The do-nothing grader has a TPR of 100% and a TNR of 0%, which says exactly
what it is. Which side counts as "positive" varies: this course follows
[Husain and Shankar's `judgy` library](https://github.com/ai-evals-course/judgy),
where a pass is positive, while
[Husain's evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) calls a
failure positive, so its TPR is the TNR here. What matters is that each
rate is computed within one side of the person's labels.

A third number, **Cohen's kappa**, compares agreement with what two raters
would reach by chance, given how often each says pass: 1 is perfect
agreement, 0 is no better than chance. It's the usual way to compare two
people's labels, where neither is the reference.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="The correctness judges, against the reading" variant="a">

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={VS_READING_DATA} setupCode={LOAD_JUDGE_LABELS + "\n\nrows = load_vs_reading()\n\n\n" + AGREEMENT_STATS} code={JUDGES_DEMO} />

*(`rows` is the first demo's, and `agreement_stats` is the exercise's
reference version, both loaded for you.)*

On their own, both judges pass nearly everything the reading passed and
catch only about half of what it failed. Most of those missed failures are
runs the reading failed for a wrong citation, which the correctness judge
was never asked about. Put
[Lesson 5's citation check](/07-evaluation/05-code-graders/03-checking-the-path-in-code/#a-check-across-the-path-and-the-reply)
in front of the judge, as
[Lesson 6's combined grader](/07-evaluation/06-model-graders/06-recap-practice/)
does, and Gemma's TNR rises from 53% to 87%, with its TPR unchanged. That's
the code-and-judge split doing what it was meant to: each misses different
failures.

These are 50 runs, so every figure is uncertain: 15 failures means a single
run moves the TNR by almost seven points. The rates also measure the whole
grader against the reading's whole-run verdict, not the judge against its
own question, which is what the labels in the next concepts are for.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="Two readers, before and after a standard" variant="b">

Kappa's natural use is two people labelling the same things. Lesson 3 had
exactly that: Simar and Claude both read 15 traces, first with no written
standard and then
[after one was written](/07-evaluation/03-error-analysis/03-whose-failure-is-it/#the-task-and-how-a-standard-decides-it).

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={READERS_DATA} setupCode={LOAD_JUDGE_LABELS + "\n\n" + AGREEMENT_STATS} code={READERS_DEMO} />

*(The skipped trace is one Claude marked "unsure".)*

Before the standard, the readers agreed on 9 of 14 traces, and a kappa of
0.19 says that's barely better than chance once their different pass rates
are accounted for. After Simar's re-review against the standard, they agree
on 12 of 14, a kappa of 0.70. The two readers didn't get better at reading.
They started answering the same question. A judge needs the same thing, and
the labels it's measured against do too: a rubric and a labelling
instruction that ask exactly what the judge is asked.

</Subsection>
