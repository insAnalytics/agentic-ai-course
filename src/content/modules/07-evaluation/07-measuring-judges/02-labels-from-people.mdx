<Subsection title="What the judges are measured against" variant="a">

[The first concept](/07-evaluation/07-measuring-judges/01-a-judge-is-a-measurement/#measured-against-what)
measured the correctness judges against Lesson 3's reading, and had to
admit the reading asked a different question. A judge has to be measured
against labels on its own question. This module's are 100 labels by
Simar, the course's author, given for that purpose:

- **Blind.** The labelling page showed the rubric and exactly what the
  judge had been shown, and nothing else: no judge verdicts, no hint of
  how the item was chosen.
- **The judge's question.** Each item asked what its judge was asked, with
  the same rubric. Correctness items had one extra control, a box to say
  the reference answer itself looked wrong, kept separate from the
  verdict.
- **Chosen to include disagreements.** Within each judge question, items
  were drawn with a fixed quota from each combination of the two judges'
  verdicts: both passed, both failed, and each way they split. So
  disagreements make up far more of the labels than of the runs, about a
  third of the correctness labels against about 8% of runs. That's good
  for finding a judge's errors, but the 100 aren't a sample of how often
  runs pass, and error rates computed from them should weight each group by
  its share of the runs. The rates in this lesson aren't weighted, which
  makes the judges look less accurate than they are.
- **Not seen before.** Runs Lesson 3's reading had already labelled were
  left out, so earlier verdicts couldn't be remembered.
- **Split before anyone looked.** Each item was assigned to development or
  test when the set was built, alternating within each group of items.

A median of under a minute an item covers a wide range: a relevance check
usually took seconds, and the slowest set F items, with several sources to
read, took several minutes. The whole set took about three and
three-quarter hours from first label to last. That's the cost
[Anthropic's guide](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
has in mind when it says to reserve systematic human studies for
calibrating the automated graders, or for outputs only people can judge,
rather than grading runs one by one.

<Subsection title="Labels turn up problems with tasks too" variant="b">

The reference-dispute box was used twice, both on the same question, q19,
which asks how to roll back a model migration. The reference answer said
you can't roll back to claude-legacy. Simar's note pointed out that the
question never mentions claude-legacy: a general answer, pausing the agent
and telling its users, is what the documents give for any rollback. The
documents bear that out, so q19's reference was changed to accept the
documented procedure, with the first one kept, and its labelled items were
left out of the test measurement because the judge had graded them
against a reference that no longer stands.

It's
[Lesson 4's rule](/07-evaluation/04-task-suite/03-conversations-and-the-simulated-user/#fixing-the-task-not-the-persona)
again, found by a different route: when a task fails good behaviour, fix
the task. A person labelling with care is often the first to notice.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="People drift too" variant="a">

["Who Validates the Validators?"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272)
(Shankar et al., UIST 2024) studied people grading model outputs to build
evaluation criteria, and found what they called criteria drift: people's
criteria change as they grade, because seeing outputs is part of how they
work out what they want. The criteria aren't fixed in advance and then
applied; grading shapes them.

This module has seen it three times:

- **Before a standard.** Lesson 3's two readers
  [agreed on 9 of the 14 traces they both decided](/07-evaluation/07-measuring-judges/01-a-judge-is-a-measurement/#two-readers-before-and-after-a-standard)
  before the standard was written down.
- **A case the rubric hadn't settled.** Lesson 3's categories put a run
  that
  [saw the write hadn't landed and explained it away](/07-evaluation/03-error-analysis/04-grouping-and-counting/#from-notes-to-categories)
  with the failures. Labelling here, the same reader passed
  [b/a13/1](/07-evaluation/06-model-graders/02-writing-a-rubric/#from-the-reading-standard-to-a-rubric),
  a reply that reports the old model and also says the change will apply
  at the next session, and the revised false-report rubric now says what
  he decided: a reply that reports the record still shows the old value
  passes, even with a wrong explanation.
- **After the development check.** The judges disagreed with two of his
  development labels, and Simar re-reviewed both:

<LiveDemo client:load dataFiles={REVISIONS_DATA} setupCode={LOAD_JUDGE_LABELS} code={REVISIONS_DEMO} />

The first is drift in its plainest form. The rubric asks how a reply
treats the question's assumption; the label answered whether the reply
helped the user, which is a better question in general and the wrong one
for this judge. The second is an ordinary slip. Both were caught only
because the development check put them in front of him again.

That raises the obvious worry: re-reviewing labels after seeing where the
judge disagrees could bend them towards the judge. Three things keep it
honest here:

- **Only development labels were re-reviewed.** The test labels were never
  shown next to a judge verdict before the test was measured.
- **The first labels are kept.** Anyone can see what changed and why, and
  recompute anything with the first pass.
- **Each change had a reason that holds without the judge.** One applied
  the rubric as written, and the other was a slip anyone would correct.

</Subsection>

<Subsection title="The same person, three weeks later" variant="b">

Criteria drift has a measurable cousin: how consistently one person labels
the same items at different times. Thirty of the 100 items, spread across
every judge question, are marked for Simar to label again at least three
weeks after the first pass, blind, in a fresh order, with nothing from the
first labelling shown. Agreement between the two passes, as kappa, says
how much of the noise in the labels is the labeller's own. That
self-agreement is a reference point for how much of any disagreement is
noise in the labels themselves.

*The relabelling is due from 23 October 2026. This section will report the
two passes' agreement and kappa on the 30 items, and what the
disagreements were, once it's done.*

</Subsection>
